Rockfish

Rockfish identification guide
(PDF, 3.49 MB)
  • Inshore benthic species
    • Mostly found in shallow depths but range from 0-300 fathoms (0-600 metres)
    • Adults live close to the bottom usually in rocky areas with high relief bottoms
    • Some species like to hide in rocky crevices

  • Midwater species
    • Mostly found in intermediate depths but range from 0-300 fathoms (0-600 metres)
    • Adults live near the bottom
    • More likely to be schooling fish
    • Most numerous near the edge of the continental shelf

  • Deep benthic species
    • Mostly found in deeper depths but range from 50-1000 fathoms (100-2000 metres)
    • Most species are red in colour
    • Mixture of on-bottom, near-bottom and off-bottom schooling species
    • Most abundant in the upper regions of the continental shelf slope

Keep an eye out for this species of Special Concern!

Rougheye / Blackspotted Complex is often misidentified as a Shortraker Rockfish which looks similar. Living up to 140 years of age, this SARA-listed species of Special Concern is possibly the longest lived fish species on earth and it needs your protection.

Total length: Up to 80 centimetres — that’s almost 3 feet!

Unique features: 2 –10 spines below the eyes and long thin gill rakers on the first gill arch

Color spectrum underwater: Pink, tan or brownish; may have brown or bronze blotches or saddles

Color spectrum above the surface: Pink or bright red with black or grey blotches.

Protect this significant species— always record and report unintended catch.

Sturgeon

Sturgeon

  • Mainly bottom dweller, found inshore and in large river systems
  • Maximum length/weight: 6 m, 600 kg
  • Long nose, projecting mouth, whiskers near tip of snout. Armour-like plates instead of scales
  • Catch and release fishing only

Sturgeon are a cartilaginous, almost prehistoric-looking fish that can grow to six metres long and weigh over 600 kg.

Their elongate body usually ranges from greenish grey on the back to light grey or white on the belly. Instead of scales, their bodies have five rows of large armour-like plates know as scutes. They have long noses and projecting mouths, located on the lower part of the head, accompanied by four fleshy whiskers known as barbels.

There are two species of sturgeon in B.C., the white sturgeon and the green sturgeon. Both occur along the coast in inshore waters and in medium to larger river systems. When observed, green sturgeon, the rarer and smaller of the species, are usually encountered further offshore.

Shellfish

Shellfish

Shellfish include all aquatic invertebrates: clams, cockles, crabs, mussels, oysters, prawns, scallops, sea urchins, and shrimp. Octopus, sea cucumbers and squid are also managed as shellfish in British Columbia.

Butter, littleneck, manila and varnish clams can be found in gravel and sand beaches in protected bays.

Razor clams live on wave-swept sand beaches open to the Pacific Ocean.

California mussel

California mussel (Mytilus californianus)
  • Key features: shell is dark blue with a heavy brown periostracum which is usually worn off except near the edge of the shell; shells are often eroded; concentric rings with coarse radial ribbing and irregular growth lines on the outer surface
  • Location: in high salinity, wave swept areas
Horse clam, aka “gapers”

Horse clam, aka “gapers” (Tresus capax)
  • Often confused with geoducks
  • Key features: much shorter siphon than geoduck with leathery plate-like flap on the tip ; shells do not completely close; clam will die if abandoned on the surface; carefully rebury these clams to the depth at which they were originally found
  • Location: low intertidal; burrow to a depth of one meter
Bent-nose macoma

Bent-nose macoma (Macoma nasuta)
  • Occasionally confused with softshell clam
  • Key features: fragile shell; posterior end of shells bend noticeably to the right; concentric rings but lacks radial ribs
  • Location: burrow up to 40 cm
Softshell clam

Softshell clam (Mya arenaria)
  • Occasionally confused with macoma clam
  • Key features: concentric rings but lacks radial ribs; the left shell of the softshell clam shell has a large, shelf-like projection at the hinge, while the right shell has a pit or depression in the same position. This projection distinguishes the softshell clam from most other Pacific Coast clams
  • Location: throughout the intertidal zone to subtidal often in estuaries, bury to 35 cm
Sickle jackknife

Sickle jackknife (Solen sicarius)
  • Occasionally confused with razor clam
  • Key features: long, cylindrical shell 4 times as long as it is wide; shell very white in colour, but covered with yellow green, and glossy periostracum
  • Location: mud flats in sheltered waters
Cockle

Cockle, aka basket cockle, heart cockle or nuttall cockle (Clinocardium nuttallii)
  • Key features: thick shell, 30–35 strong rounded, radial ribs; the radial ribs interlock as undulations between the two shells; elongated foot which can exceed the length of the shell when fully extended
  • Location: just below surface of substrate
Butter clam

Butter clam (Saxidomus gigantea)
  • Occasionally confused with Manila or littleneck clams
  • Key features: heavy and solid; prominent concentric rings but lacks radial ribs, hinge ligament is large and brown; siphon can be retracted into the shell only with difficulty; moderate gape at posterior end
  • Location: low intertidal habitats
Geoduck

Geoduck (Panopea generosa)
  • Key features: larger and more subtidal than a horse clam; shells are somewhat rectangular in shape with a very long protruding neck and siphon; long-lived, with maximum ages of at least 168 years
  • Location: subtidal zone, burrow to a depth of one meter
Littleneck clam

Littleneck clam (Protothaca staminea)
  • Easily confused with Manila clams
  • Key features: lower side of the hinge of the native littleneck clam typically has a pronounced ridge you can feel with your finger; rounder than Manila clam, concentric rings and radial ribs
  • Location: mid to low intertidal habitats, burrow up to 10 cm
Manila clam

Manila clam (Venerupis philippinarum)
  • Easily confused with littleneck clams
  • Key features: lower side of the hinge of the Manila clam is indented you can feel with your finger; shell slightly oblong; concentric rings and radial ribs; exterior colour is variable, with angular patterns; interior of the shell has purple or yellow
  • Location: high in the intertidal, burrow up to 10 cm
Varnish clam, aka savoury clam

Varnish clam, aka savoury clam (Nuttallia obscurata)
  • Key features: a thick, shiny periostracum; purple coloration on the interior of the shell, round
  • Location: sand/gravel beaches, often in areas of freshwater runoff or groundwater seepage
Pacific razor clam

Pacific razor clam (Siliqua patula)
  • Occasionally confused with sickle jackknife
  • Key features: shells are long but oval shaped 2.5 times as long as it is wide; smooth concentric rings, lacks radial ribs; highly polished, light to dark olive periostracum capable of re-burrowing
  • Location: exposed sandy coasts, very low intertidal area, burrow deeply
Blue mussel

Blue mussel (Mytilus edulis)
  • Key features: long, teardrop shaped, hinged shell with rounded edges; shell is dark blue/black; concentric rings but lacks radiating ribs; clings to hard surfaces (rocks, logs, structures) by a cluster of byssus threads
  • Location: calm waters often at the highest of the intertidal levels
Pacific Oyster

Pacific Oyster (Crassostrea gigas)
  • Key features: shells are unequal in size and shape; generally elongated, oblong, and cupped but can be variable depending on substrate; shells are chalky white or gray; thick, rough, and sometimes sharp shells; irregular, rounded, radial folds on the shell
  • Location: attached to rocks, debris, or other oysters
Olympia oyster

Olympia oyster (Ostrea lurida)
  • Species of Special Concern under the Species at Risk Act. To prevent the accidental harvest of Olympia oysters it is recommended to only harvest Pacific oysters with a minimum size of 5 cm
  • Key features: white to dark purple; can be distinguished from Pacific oysters by little ridges along the hinge; variable shape but often more round in appearance than the Pacific oyster
  • Location: found on hard substrates in lower intertidal and shallow subtidal zones
Abalone

Abalone
  • Thin, oval shell with irregular reddish or greenish upper surface
  • Shell sometimes marked with blue or white. Often encrusted with organisms
  • Iridescent white shell interior with faint pink and green sheen
  • Series of three to six raised holes to allow water currents to pass over gills

    • Northern abalone is listed as endangered under Canada’s Species at Risk Act. It is illegal to fish for abalone, to harm or harass them or to possess any abalone or part of abalone, including the shell.
Shrimp

Shrimp
  • Coonstripe shrimp: Red-brown. Irregular brown and white oblique lines on shell and abdomen. Found in shallow water on or near pilings and floats
  • Humpback shrimp: Red-tan. Dark red and bright white markings. Found on soft or hard bottom
  • Pink shrimp: Uniform translucent red. Live on soft bottom
Prawn

Prawn
  • Red. White horizontal bars on shell, at least two pairs of white spots on abdomen
  • Found in rocky areas
  • Largest shrimp in B.C., up to 25 cm
Squid

Squid
  • Soft-bodied swimmer. Muscular tube (mantle) has fins at narrow end, contains internal organs
  • Mantle supports head (with large eyes), arms, two feeding tentacles with suckers and sometimes hooks on ends
  • Opal squid: Pale lavender. Size: up to 13 cm in B.C. Found in inshore waters. Often gather in large schools to spawn in shallow water
  • Neon flying squid: Mauve on upper surface, silvery or gold below. Tentacle sucker rings have 4 large teeth with many smaller teeth between. Size: up to 100 cm, 5 kg
  • Humboldt squid: Brown or red. Size: up to 200 cm, 45 kg
Crab

Crab

It is illegal to possess female crabs.

How to identify a crab

  • 3 to 4 pairs of walking legs
  • 1 pair of claws for feeding and defence
  • Female carries egg clusters on underside of body

Crab sex is determined by looking at the abdomen shape

  • Female’s abdomen has a wide “beehive” shape
  • Male’s abdomen has a narrow “lighthouse” shape
Species Characteristics
Brown Box Crab

Brown Box Crab
  • Rounded bumpy, and typically reddish-brown or tan with purplish and white areas
  • Legs are shorter in proportion to body size, fitting tightly to body when folded in
  • Carapace width can reach 18.5 cm (7.3 inches)
  • Claws fold tightly against body
  • Covered in tubercles and spines, giving rough armored look
Puget Sound King Crab

Puget Sound King Crab
  • Bumpy, rounded, and multicoloured – typically red, orange and purple
  • Shorter legs in proportion to body size compared to other king crabs
  • Carapace width can reach 30cm (12 inches)
  • Covered in blunt, rounded bumps
  • Juveniles typically bright orange sometimes with red and purple patches
  • Relatively uncommon to catch
Alaskan King Crab

Alaskan King Crab
  • Thick, spiny, and typically reddish-brown
  • Hard, rough shell with rugged look due to bumps and spikes
  • Can weight over 10 kg (22lbs)
  • Long legs, leg span reaching up to 1.8 meters (6 ft)
  • One claw is noticeably larger, used for crushing prey
Dungeness crab

Dungeness crab
  • Medium to large-sized crab with shell width typically ranging from 15-20 cm (6 to 8 inches)
  • Shell is hard and rounded with smooth texture
  • Typically reddish-brown to purple on top and lighter underneath
  • white-tipped claws
  • Broad, oval shaped body with prominent legs
Redrock crab

Redrock crab
  • Brick-red carapace with a lighter, whitish underside
  • Smooth and fan-like shell
  • Black-tipped claws
  • Carapace can reach up to 160mm (6.3 inches)
European green crab

European green crab
  • Adult size: up to 10 cm
  • Shell serrated and trapezoid-shaped, with three spines between the eyes and five on each side
  • Colour variable: green, red or yellow

    • European green crab is an invasive species.
