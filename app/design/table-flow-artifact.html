How a sheet is built

Rules reach a water from four directions: the province’s, the region’s, the river’s own, and those inherited from a river it flows into. This is how they are sorted, how they are shown together, and how a reader can tell which one is actually governing them — from the simplest case to the hardest one in the corpus.

The one idea

Rules are sorted by what they are, never by who wrote them.

A provincial hook rule and a river’s own hook rule are the same kind of statement, so they land in the same table, next to each other. That is the whole mechanism: you can only see that a river has narrowed a regional default if both are in front of you.

Where every kind of rule goes

Fifteen kinds of statement, 2,396 rules in the shipped corpus. The counts are of rules, not of appearances — a provincial rule reaches thousands of waters and is counted once. The column sums to 2,396: every rule in the corpus is accounted for, and nothing falls through.

How to check this. The nine cases below are drawn from thirteen rivers rendered end to end — Fraser, Chilliwack, Harrison, Fording, Atnarko, Bella Coola, Skeena, Elk, Coquihalla, Kootenay, Okanagan, Babine and the Yakoun on Haida Gwaii. Every sentence quoted is the verbatim stored on the rule, every field is the value in the shipped bundle, and every output is what the page renders today.

case 2The river narrows its region
Region 4 allows five trout a day; the Elk River’s own row says one, between 15 June and 31 October. Both are in the table and the reader is told which is which by the last column. The region’s number is the headline and the river’s is indented beneath it — deliberately: the general rule states the default, and the specific one is a narrowing of it. Reading down the group gives “trout and char are five a day in Region 4 — but on this water, one, in season”. Nothing is struck, because neither rule is wrong; one is simply more local, which the page knows from scope.

case 6An exemption that narrows but does not lift
The same field as case 5, the opposite outcome — and this is the one that has to be got right. when_targeting: [WSG] means the hole is only for sturgeon, so the ban stands and the exemption keeps its own line beneath it. Striking the parent would tell an angler the fin-fish bait ban is gone, when it is gone for one quarry on three named rivers. Sixty rules in the corpus carry an exemption — 53 lift the rule they name outright, 7 only cut a hole in it. Note the last column: the exemption reaches the Fraser by naming it, so it reads this water, while the ban it qualifies is provincial.

case 9Two regions, and neither one wins
For 29 km the Fraser is the boundary between Region 5 and Region 7A, one region on each bank, and the reach carries management units from both. Region 7A imposes a bait ban and a spring closure there that Region 5 does not. There is no narrower rule to choose, so both sets are shown, neither is struck, and the page says the stricter reading is the safe one. Splitting the reach in half lengthwise would have been tidier and would have told an angler on one bank that a bait ban they are standing under does not exist.

How you can tell

Three marks, and they are the whole answer.

  • The last column names the level. “this water” means the synopsis wrote it for this river; “Region 8” and “Provincial” mean it reaches you by containing you; and “(as a tributary)” means it is a larger river’s rule that reaches here because this water flows into it — the Fording’s only licence is the Elk’s, and the Fording’s own page in the book does not carry it.
  • Indentation means narrowing. A row set in from the one above is a condition on it, not a separate limit.
  • A struck number or sentence has been overruled — and whatever overruled it is on the same line, so you never have to go looking for the reason.
And the limit, stated plainly on the sheet itself. A river’s rule replaces a regional default only where the two can be matched on what they control. Where they cannot, both rows are shown and neither is struck — so read the river’s own rows before relying on a regional number. The page never drops a rule to make two of them agree.

What decides all of this

Every decision above reads a field on the rule. None reads the words of a sentence — a claim that is checkable, because a text test looks like /…/.test(label) and the page contains none.

The fields read are: type, family, dimension, species, take, may_target, unlimited, over_cm, under_cm, combined, within, origin, period, water, windows, scope, via, permitted, method, record_retention, on_retention, when_open, water_class, angler_class, document, grantor, when_targeting and exempts. Fifteen entry ids are named in one lookup table, for sentences that qualify a specific rule rather than describing one.

A rule’s wording is generated from its fields, so matching text against it is a round trip through every phrasing decision the generator makes — a test that keeps passing until someone rewords a sentence, then fails silently. It had already happened once: the Fording River’s only licence printed on no row at all, because the check looked for “A … is required” at the start of a label that begins “Class II water — …”. Four such tests existed; all four are now fields, and in three cases that meant correcting the data rather than teaching the page to guess.

The three judgements are the ones worth arguing with. Two are ordering, and ordering cannot make a sheet wrong — every rule is still on it. The third hides something: “fishing through ice is permitted” carries no water type, so the corpus does not say it is a lake rule. That is domain knowledge written into the page rather than pretended into the data, and it is guarded so it can only ever hide a permission — never a prohibition.
