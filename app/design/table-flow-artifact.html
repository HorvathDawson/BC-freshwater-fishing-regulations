How a sheet is built

Every table on a water’s screen is produced by the same short pipeline, and every decision in it keys on a rule’s type or one of its fields. Nothing keys on what a sentence happens to say. This is the whole flow, with the places where a judgement was made rather than a fact read — so those can be argued with.

The flow

One list of rules comes in. It is split by what each rule is, never by where it came from — so a river’s own rule and a provincial default land in the same table and can be compared there.

What decides what

Three kinds of decision, and they are worth telling apart. A rule reads a field and holds for any input. A lookup names specific entries. A judgement is something the corpus does not say and someone chose.

The three judgements are the ones to argue with. Two are ordering, and ordering cannot make a sheet wrong — every rule is still on it. The third hides something: “fishing through ice is permitted” carries no water type, so the corpus does not say it is a lake rule; moving water not carrying safe ice is domain knowledge, written in the page rather than pretended into the data. It is guarded so it can only ever hide a permission — a prohibition is never hidden by it.

Why the sentence is never read

A rule’s label is generated from its fields. Matching text against it is a round trip through prose and through every wording choice the generator makes — which is a test that keeps passing until someone rewords a sentence, and then fails silently.

It had already happened. licenceRowOf looked for “A … is required” at the start of the label, and the Fording River’s only licence begins “Class II water — …”. It matched nothing, so the row printed nowhere at all, on a water where a Classified Waters Licence is required.

Four such tests existed. Each is now a field, and in three cases that meant fixing the data rather than teaching the view to guess:

  • “no general minimum size limit for trout in lakes” was hidden on rivers by matching the phrase “in lakes”. It carries water: "lake".
  • “never have in your possession more than the quota” was separated from its entry-mate by matching the word “possession”. It carries period: "possession".
  • “Permission of the landowner is required” was re-read to find out whose permission. It carries grantor: "landowner".
  • and the sentence that closes the method list is picked out by its rule’s identity — a sentence can be reworded; a rule cannot be renamed without someone noticing.

What this does not promise

A water’s rule replaces a regional default only where the two can be matched on what they control. Where they cannot, both rows are shown and neither is struck — the sheet says so above the tables, and that is the honest state, not a hedge. The page never silently drops a rule to make two of them agree.

The other standing limit is upstream of all of this: a rule only reaches a stretch if the atlas could place it. Rules naming a spot the map cannot draw are carried on the water in the book’s own words, with no stretch attached, rather than dropped — because on a closure, silence reads as permission.
