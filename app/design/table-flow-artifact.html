How a sheet is built

Three levels of rule reach every water in British Columbia: the province’s, the region’s, and the river’s own. This is how they are sorted, how they are shown together, and how a reader can tell which one is actually governing them — from the simplest case to the hardest one in the corpus.

The one idea

Rules are sorted by what they are, never by who wrote them.

A provincial hook rule and a river’s own hook rule are the same kind of statement, so they land in the same table, next to each other. That is the whole mechanism: you can only see that a river has narrowed a regional default if both are in front of you.

Fifteen kinds of statement in the corpus. Every one has a home, and nothing lands in more than one place by accident.

Sixteen sentences are deliberately not shown. They are notices that instruct the reader to do by hand what the app has already done — “check the tributaries of the following lakes for special regulations” — or that carry nothing to act on, like “this is the only white sturgeon fishery in the province’s non-tidal waters”. Each is listed by name in the code, so the set can be argued with.

case 6An exemption that narrows but does not lift
The same field, the opposite outcome — and this is the one that has to be got right. when_targeting: [WSG] means the hole is only for sturgeon, so the ban stands and the exemption keeps its own line. Striking the parent would tell an angler the fin-fish bait ban is gone, when it is gone for one quarry on three named rivers. Three of the six exemptions in the corpus are full lifts; three are narrowings.

case 9Two regions, and neither one wins
For 29 km the Fraser is the boundary between Region 5 and Region 7A, one region on each bank, and the reach carries management units from both. Region 7A imposes a bait ban and a spring closure there that Region 5 does not. There is no narrower rule to choose, so both sets are shown, neither is struck, and the page says the stricter reading is the safe one. Splitting the reach in half lengthwise would have been tidier and would have told an angler on one bank that a bait ban they are standing under does not exist.

How you can tell

Three marks, and they are the whole answer.

  • The last column names the level. “this water” means the synopsis wrote it for this river. “Region 8” and “Provincial” mean it reaches you by containing you.
  • Indentation means narrowing. A row set in from the one above is a condition on it, not a separate limit.
  • A struck number or sentence has been overruled — and whatever overruled it is on the same line, so you never have to go looking for the reason.
And the limit, stated plainly on the sheet itself. A river’s rule replaces a regional default only where the two can be matched on what they control. Where they cannot, both rows are shown and neither is struck — so read the river’s own rows before relying on a regional number. The page never drops a rule to make two of them agree.

A rule’s wording is generated from its fields, so matching text against it is a round trip through every phrasing decision the generator makes — a test that keeps passing until someone rewords a sentence, then fails silently. It had already happened once: the Fording River’s only licence printed on no row at all, because the check looked for “A … is required” at the start of a label that begins “Class II water — …”. Four such tests existed; all four are now fields, and in three cases that meant correcting the data rather than teaching the page to guess.

The three judgements are the ones worth arguing with. Two are ordering, and ordering cannot make a sheet wrong — every rule is still on it. The third hides something: “fishing through ice is permitted” carries no water type, so the corpus does not say it is a lake rule. That is domain knowledge written into the page rather than pretended into the data, and it is guarded so it can only ever hide a permission — never a prohibition.
