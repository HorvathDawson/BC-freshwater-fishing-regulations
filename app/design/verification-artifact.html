What this pass found

A row marked [Includes Tributaries] puts its rules on the whole catchment above the water — tens of thousands of sections from one line of print. Some of those lines then name what they do not cover. The sentence is recorded; the carve-out it asks for is a curator’s job, because “upstream from Kootenay Lake to the U.S. border” is a reach somebody has to point at, and the field for it is still empty on these rows.

Nothing about a single sheet can show this. On the excepted water the rule renders like any other, correctly traced to a passage that says, in its own words, that it does not apply there. Only the pair gives it away.

How a row in the book finds its water

Before any of the checking below means anything, the right water has to have been chosen — and that happens in one place, the matcher, from the heading the book prints. These are its results over the whole synopsis, not a sample.

The split matters for review. A row matched by name resolved to exactly one registry item on its own evidence; nobody decided anything, and nothing has to be re-checked when the registry moves. A row matched by override is a judgement a person made about which water the book meant — two lakes share a name, or one heading covers several waters, or the book writes one polygon as three. Those are the rows to read first, and every sheet below names the ones reaching it.

How a conflict gets decided

Two rules reach the same water and say different things. Six ways that happens, each with the case it was worked out on — because the rule for resolving them has to come from the data, not from taste.
