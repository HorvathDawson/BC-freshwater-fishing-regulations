One Interface, Two Storages

One interface, two storages.

How the regulations, the geometry, the gauges and the stocking get to a phone with no signal and to a browser that must answer in one round trip — measured against the real build, not estimated from the shape of the problem.

Written against build 54ea0bb4 · 2 053 299 sections · 19 862 registry items · 30 Aug 2026

The whole thing in six lines

Tiles hold the shapes of rivers and their names. Nothing else.
The bundle is one small database holding every rule, every name, and the few things only the build can work out.
Feeds are tiny files that change often — gauge readings, stocking, closures — and they join to the bundle by one id.
The phone keeps all of it. The browser keeps none of it and reads pieces on demand.
Both go through the same set of functions, so they cannot answer differently.
Everything below is the evidence for those five sentences.

02Four artifacts, two version domains

Doc 13 §1.1 established three data classes. Delivering them takes four artifacts, because geometry and regulations change on the same clock but have byte budgets three orders of magnitude apart.

2,053,299sections in the graph
84,420carry a registry item — 4.1%
7,614rule → section bindings province-wide
60,173named sections — 2.9%

That first pair of numbers is the load-bearing measurement in this document. Regulation data is tiny and geometry is enormous. 96% of the atlas will never have a rule written about it; it exists so the map looks like the country. Any design that carries regulations inside the geometry pays the 2-million-row cost to deliver a 7,614-row fact.

ArtifactHoldsVersion domainChanges
Tiles waters.pmtilesgeometry, names, aliases, order, magnitudebundleper full build
Bundle regs.sqliteitems, rules, entries, verbatim, climatology, precomputesbundleper build + per edition
Feeds feed/*gauge, stocking, in-season notices, DFO salmonone per feed30 min – weekly
Blobsbathymetric sheets, synopsis page imagesimmutable URLnever

The rule that keeps the two domains apart

Tiles and bundle are content-addressed and pinned to each other; the client refuses a mixed pair. Feeds are versioned independently and join to the bundle by item_id or station_id — never by section_id, never by a build-time match table. A 30-minute gauge tick must not re-version anything a client has cached.

Tiles carry geometry and identity. Nothing else — no regulation, no reading, no stocking date, no colour. This is not tidiness: it is what makes the map free to recolour.

Two name fields, not one

name is for the map and must look right: Wahleach Lake. alt is for search and must match anything a person types. Fusing them gives you either an ugly map or a search that misses. The build normalises case, strips the registry's "EAST WHITE RIVER"/"East White River" duplicates, and only emits alt when it carries something the name does not.

Zoom buckets come from magnitude, not Strahler order

Corrected. Strahler order jumps in big steps — 578 features, then 10,767, then 48,107 — so a zoom ladder built on it either shows almost nothing or almost everything. Magnitude (the count of headwater streams above a reach) is nearly continuous, so the ladder is smooth and it drops small tributaries while keeping whole big rivers, which is what v1's atlas tiles did and why they looked right.

Correction: named water is not exempt. Drawing every named creek at z6 would look terrible and defeats the point of a ladder. Searchability does not need the geometry rendered — the 90 KB name index in §8 covers every named water at every zoom, and tapping a result flies the map to it. Names respect the same magnitude cut as everything else.

Unnamed headwaters are the whole cost

1,993,126 of the 2,053,299 sections have no name. They exist so the map looks like the country, and they only appear at z14. If the tile build comes in too large, this is the dial: cutting unnamed water at z14 to mag ≥ 2 removes 1.45 million features and nothing a person can search for.

Polygons need their own ladder

Magnitude is a line attribute. Lakes, wetlands and admin boundaries cut on area, and depth contours on their own index interval — a 1 m contour is z14 detail, a 10 m contour belongs several zooms earlier. Contours also cut out entirely below the zoom where a lake is bigger than a few pixels.

Colour comes from feature-state, never from the tile

This is already the contract in packages/map/src/style.ts: every non-static colour mode reads ["feature-state", property]. The client sets state for the features in view from the bundle and the feeds. Switching from Regulations to Conditions, changing the date, or flipping theme therefore never refetches a tile.

// the join, in three lines
tile feature  →  f.id        dense section index
bundle        →  status(item, date)  → 'closed' | 'limited' | 'open' | 'unknown'
map.setFeatureState({ source:'waters', id:f.id }, { status, standing })

The one thing dense ids cost

A dense index renumbers on every build, so tiles + spine precomputes must re-ship whole. That is fine — they re-ship anyway. It is the regulatory layer that needs deltas, and it keys on item_id, which doc 13 §0.1 measured at 99.88% stable across a rebuild. The dense id never leaves the tile/precompute pair; it is a packing detail, exactly as doc 13 §3 requires.

I had v1's bloat wrong

I said admin boundaries caused it. They did not — only 25,245 of 2,053,299 sections sit inside any admin area at all (1.2%), across 167 areas, and only 9 sit in two. The largest is Gwaii Haanas at 4,207 sections.

The bloat came from zone regulations giving every stream a regulation automatically. That is a different problem and it needs a different fix. (Buffers are gone; the Liard watershed case is a single regulation over a large area, which the mechanism below handles as one row.)

How a zone rule reaches two million streams without being written down two million times

The whole trick is that the scope is a property of the geometry, not of the rule. Three small integers on the tile feature, and three small tables in the bundle:

// on the tile feature — administrative GEOGRAPHY, true regardless of any rule
  mus   uint16[]  every management unit this section touches. Usually one.

// in the BUNDLE — membership of a REGULATED area is regulation data, not geography
section_area(section_id, area_id)         25,245 rows today

// in the bundle — the ENTIRE regulation index, three columns
reg_index(scope_kind, scope_id, rule_id)
   scope_kind = section   scope_id = section id   7,614 rows
              | mu        scope_id = mu number    a few hundred rows
              | area      scope_id = area id      a few dozen rows

Why mus is on the tile and area is not

Corrected. A management unit is administrative geography — every square metre of BC is in one, whether or not anybody regulates it. Putting it on the feature leaks nothing.

A regulated area is different: we only carry the areas that are regulated, so "this section is in area 7" is a regulation fact wearing a geometry costume. It belongs in the bundle. It is also cheap there — 25,245 rows, about 150 KB.

The park and reserve polygons still belong in the tile, because a park boundary is useful cartography on its own. Draw more of them than we regulate and the leak disappears entirely: the layer stops being "the regulated ones" and becomes "protected land".

A section can touch more than one MU, so mus is a list

And that raises the real question: what colour is a stretch that runs through two management units with different rules?

OptionCostVerdict
Split every stream at every MU boundary more sections; and geometry now depends on an administrative line No — see below
Colour the most restrictive MU it touches, and say so one extra sentence in the sheetYes
Split only where the MUs actually disagree this edition geometry would depend on regulations No — that is the coupling this whole document exists to avoid

Why not split. Two reasons, and the measurement settles the first.

It is 0.8% of the atlas. Re-cutting two million sections to fix 16,701 of them is not a trade — it is a rounding error paid for with the whole geometry.

And MU boundaries get redrawn. Bake them into geometry and a ministry revision re-cuts the atlas and renumbers every section downstream, invalidating the tiles, the precomputes and every saved pin. Keep MU as an attribute and the same revision is a re-stamp: same geometry, same ids, one column rewritten.

What the sheet says when they disagree — and it must, because the map can only show one colour:

  This stretch runs through MU 2-2 and MU 2-3.
  In 2-2 it is closed until 15 June. In 2-3 it is open.
  The map shows the stricter one.

Colouring a feature is then: look up its section rules, the rules for each MU in its mus list, and its area rules; evaluate each against the date and the species group in core/; take the most restrictive. A spring closure covering all of MU 4-5 is one row. It colours 60,000 streams because 60,000 features carry 45 in their mus list, not because anyone wrote 60,000 rows.

The Liard is the worst case, and it is the right thing to benchmark against

It is the largest regulated area in the province, it is not yet built, and it is exactly the stress test this mechanism needs. The watersheds layer already has it:

142,173 km²Liard River watershed
322,626sections inside it — 15.7% of the atlas
1.58 MBof section_area rows
1row in reg_index — the rule itself

So no, it does not blow it up — and that is now measured rather than hoped. Total section_area goes from 25,245 rows to about 348,000, under 2 MB inside a ~10 MB bundle. Adding it gives the bundle a realistic worst case to be sized against, which is worth more than the 1.58 MB costs.

Some areas cut the streams that cross them; some do not. The switch already exists.

pipeline/atlas/build.py, in the blanket-area pass:

if ad.get("cut", True):        # `cut` flag is the SOLE cut trigger (default on)
    apts = resolve_area_splits(polys, chains)
    split_graph_at(...)
else:
    print(f"  area '{ad['id']}': membership-only (no cut)")

So adding the Liard costs zero extra sections: one entry in areas.json with "cut": false.

And cutting would buy nothing there. A park boundary is an arbitrary line that streams cross constantly, so cutting is what makes "inside the park" precise. A watershed boundary is a drainage divide — by definition water does not cross it. The few exceptions (canals, and where the polygon follows the provincial border) are already covered: mark_inside_areas runs two passes, covers for the clean case and intersects for straddlers. Membership never needed the cut; the cut is only what makes the first pass exact.

Format now, contents later

Those three columns are the contract. What goes in them, and how the pipeline decides, is deliberately not settled here — zone regs need cleaning up first, and area regs need a rule for how they reach unnamed streams. The client does not care: it reads three columns and evaluates dates. Anything the pipeline learns later slots in without a schema change.

We already have the polygons — I was wrong

I said MU came only from the synopsis entry and that we would have to go and fetch boundaries. data/fetch_data.py already pulls them: wmu, 225 polygons, keyed 1-15, 2-8 and so on, sitting in data/bc_fisheries_data.gpkg beside a watersheds layer of 11,580 named basins. Stamping is a build step, not a data-acquisition problem.

Measured: what MU stamping costs

2,069,296(section, MU) rows for 2,053,306 sections
99.14%touch exactly one MU
16,505touch two — 0.80% of the atlas
196touch three or more

Coverage is 100% — every section lands in an MU. And because 99.14% have exactly one, the tile field is a single uint16 for almost every feature, with a short list only for the 16,701 that straddle.

Where they sit in the sheet: one stack, most-specific first, each tier labelled in the reader's language — Written for this water, then Everywhere in MU 2-2, then Everywhere in BC. A person should never have to wonder whether they have seen all of it, and the tiers are never merged into one undifferentiated list.

Two field names worth explaining plainly

  • tombstone — when a rebuild deletes or merges a registry item, its old id must not just vanish. A saved spot pointing at it should say "this water is now part of Chilliwack River", not fail silently or, worse, show the wrong water. The row is (old_id → reason, replaced_by) and it is the difference between a rebuild being safe and a rebuild quietly breaking people's pins.
  • clause_start, clause_len — the character range inside the synopsis paragraph that a rule was parsed out of, so the app can highlight it. The prototype re-finds it at runtime with a whitespace-tolerant regex, which works 49 times out of 49 today and is a silent failure waiting to happen. The parser already knows the offset; write it down.

Magnitude and order are not in the bundle

Corrected. They live on the tile feature, which the client already has when you tap. The only build-time use for magnitude is computing gauge representativeness, and that result is stored in section_gauge. Nothing needs a second copy.

05Live feeds

Corrected. The current reading belongs in the same file as the series it belongs to. Splitting them meant a station's panel needed two fetches for one story. There are exactly two gauge artifacts:

The station's own envelope rides in its file, so opening a gauge is one fetch. The bundle still carries gauge_clim for all 450 stations because the map needs a percentile for water it has not opened — but that copy is the compressed pentad form below, and the per-station file carries the fuller one.

Putting pctile and standing in the index — not just discharge — is what lets the map colour every watershed without loading any climatology. The publisher computes it once; every client agrees by construction.

The climatology compresses, and by exactly how much is measurable

A full day-of-year envelope is 366 days × 7 percentiles × 450 stations. Stored naïvely that is 4.5 MB. Percentile-versus-day curves are smooth, so they sample:

SchemeSizeMean errorWorstVerdict
Daily, float32, p0–p1004,503 KB——baseline
Pentad, p0–p100455 KB1.02%82%rejected
Pentad, p10–p90 only325 KB0.44%5.6%ship this
Weekly, p10–p90232 KB1.00%11.1%too coarse

Where the 82% came from, and why it decided the schema

Every worst case was p100 — the single wettest day ever recorded for that date. A record maximum is one storm; it is not a smooth function of day-of-year and it does not interpolate. p10–p90 are smooth and compress to 0.44% mean error; the two extremes are not and must not be sampled. They are cosmetic (the outer band of the seasonal chart), so they ship per-day for the ~40 stations a client actually opens, or not at all. This is the kind of thing you only find by measuring, and it is a schema decision, not a tuning one.

Stocking

Two artifacts for the same reason as gauges: the map needs one number per lake, the sheet needs a history, and the history is 200× larger.

feed/stocking/last.json        one row per stocked waterbody, weekly
  { wb: "00394CRYL", item: 4821, d: "2026-04-02", sp: 3, q: 500, st: 2 }
  ~1,000 BC waterbodies × ~28 B  ≈  28 KB   — always resident

feed/stocking/{waterbody}.json  full release history, on tap
  Deka Lake alone has 179 releases; the province has ~200,000

The map layer wants recency, so last.json carries the date and the client derives the band. Species and life-stage are small integer codes against a dictionary in the bundle — "Rainbow Trout" repeated 200,000 times is 3.4 MB of the same string.

Bathymetric charts — a list, not a layer

Two kinds, and the difference matters to a person standing at a lake:

lake_sheet(item_id, sheet_no, title, scale, drafted, kind, url, bytes)
  kind = "scan"    a PDF of the 1974–1996 survey sheet — 738 MB province-wide, stays remote
  kind = "vector"  a digitised contour set — becomes a tile layer, ships with the map

The list is in the bundle, so the map can mark which lakes have a chart at all (2,744 province-wide) without holding a byte of the charts. Digitised contours live in atlas.pmtiles and grow as more get vectorised, with no schema change — doc 13 §1.3's test passing.

The scans are downloadable, and the map says which you have

A PDF is useless on a lake with no signal, so they are downloadable — one lake at a time, or a whole region in one go. The depth layer then has three states, and that is the layer's whole job:

Which is on device is client state, not bundle state — it is the one thing in this document that differs per install.

In-season notices, and DFO salmon — which is a different thing entirely

Corrected, and this one matters. I put DFO salmon in the precedence chain. It does not belong there. The provincial synopsis governs trout, char, whitefish, coarse fish; DFO governs salmon. They are two authorities over two different sets of species, so a DFO closure does not override a provincial rule — it answers a question the provincial rule never asked.

A river can be open for rainbow trout under BC rules and closed for coho under DFO rules on the same afternoon. Rendering that as one colour is how somebody gets fined.

FeedCadenceGovernsRelationship
feed/inseason.json6 hthe same species the synopsis does overrides — it amends a rule already shipped
feed/dfo.jsondailysalmon only parallel — a second answer, never merged with the first

Precedence, corrected

Two independent stacks, both evaluated by one function in @app/core:

// non-salmon species — trout, char, whitefish, coarse fish
provincial base_reg  <  zone reg (MU)  <  entry rule  <  in-season notice

// salmon — an entirely separate authority
DFO opening/closure

So status() takes a species group and returns one answer per stack. On the map that means a species selector, not a blended colour: the default view colours by the provincial stack, and a Salmon view colours by DFO. Rule 23 in AGENTS.md already forbids two surfaces inventing different vocabularies for the same state; this makes sure they are not answering different questions either.

These override reaches, not whole waters

A notice says "the Chilliwack downstream of Vedder Crossing", which is a stretch, not a river. But a feed must never depend on the graph. So the publisher resolves the extent — it runs the same resolver the pipeline does, in about a tenth of a second for the whole corpus — and publishes item_id + the section list it resolved against + the bundle version. If a client is on a different build, it falls back to the whole item and says so. Conservative, never silently wrong, and the publisher still does not need to be clever.

section_down: you were right, cut it

The only thing it does is trace a tap down to its gauge. Every reach on that path is, by definition, inside the same gauge's watershed — so storing the pointer for shed members alone is not a compromise, it is complete. That is ~300k rows instead of 2.02 million: 8.1 MB → 1.2 MB, and nothing is lost. There is no second use for the other 1.7 million pointers.

place_water: my arithmetic was wrong, and you were right to just compute it

I said 1,624 × 2 million and called it too big. That is the wrong second number. You do not measure to every section, you measure to every named waterbody — and after deduplication there are about 20,000 of those. 1,624 × 20,000 = 32.5 million distance tests, which is seconds of work, not a reason to approximate.

So: compute the full matrix, keep everything within 25 km, sorted by distance. The result is small because most towns have nothing near them — a few hundred thousand rows province-wide. Web shards it one small file per town, fetched when that town is tapped; mobile keeps one table. Same interface either way, which is what §7 is for.

Colour-blind mode is a theme, not a special case. The style system already resolves colours from named tokens per theme (resolveTheme), so a cvd theme swaps the red/green pair for blue/orange and nothing else changes. Two further rules make it work without relying on hue at all: outcome also carries a dash pattern (solid, dashed, dotted), and the sheet always states the outcome as a word. Rule 29 already forbids a colour mode that leaves an enum member uncoloured, so unknown cannot be dropped from the CVD theme by accident.

packages/data/src/index.ts says the query shapes are placeholders and must not be guessed. They are no longer guesses — §1 is the evidence. Every method below appears because a screen asks for it, and every one goes into the conformance suite in the same commit.

Two shapes carry the whole design

regsForItem returns the sheet in one call — the client never assembles a screen from six queries, because on a phone that is six page faults and on the web it is six round trips. statusFor takes an array of section indices, because the map colours a viewport, not a feature. Everything else is a leaf.

How an installed app stays current

Three different problems wearing one word. Separating them is what stops "update" meaning "download 800 MB on cellular".

WhatWhenBytesAsks permission?
Feeds — gauges, stocking, in-season, DFO on app open, then every 30 min while connected and foregrounded ~120 KBno
Regulatory delta — an edition amendment on app open, when bundle.version moved tens of KBno
Spine — a new build: tiles + precomputes when the app is told the build changed hundreds of MByes — and only on wifi by default

Deltas over R2, without inventing a protocol

R2 speaks HTTP range requests, which is enough for all three:

  • Feeds are whole small files. If-None-Match on the ETag; a 304 costs nothing. No delta needed.
  • Regulatory deltas key on item_id, which doc 13 measured at 99.88% stable. Publish delta/{from}-{to}.json — the rows that changed. If the client's version is too old to have a delta path, it refetches the whole 4 MB, which is fine.
  • Tiles are the hard case, and the honest answer is do not delta them. PMTiles is a sorted immutable file; a new build reshuffles it. But a build happens rarely (months), it is already a deliberate, permissioned, wifi-only download, and the client can keep serving the old tiles until the new set is fully in place. Atomic swap, never a partial state.

The one refinement worth building: publish tiles per region as well as whole. Not to make the initial install smaller — the province ships — but so a rebuild that only touched Region 2 can re-download one region instead of all eight.

09Freshness and precedence

@app/core already has freshness(), and its comment states the rule correctly: a stale answer must never render as a live one. Two extensions make it complete.

/** Distinguishing these three is a correctness requirement, not a nicety. */
type Status =
  | { outcome: "closed" | "limited" | "open"; provenance: Provenance }
  | { outcome: "unknown"; because: "unplaceable" | "near-name" | "feed-unreachable" };

type Provenance = "written-for-this-water" | "general-rules-only";

Splitting outcome from provenance is doc 13 §2.1's default_only fix, and the prototype proves it renders: colour is the outcome, weight is the provenance. A water with no rule of its own is open and drawn as a hairline; it is never a fourth colour.

The three unknown reasons are separate because they need separate words. "A rule names something spelled almost like this water" and "we could not reach the notice feed" are both unknown, and telling a person which one is the difference between a useful app and a shrug.

10Decisions, and the one thing still open

Range-read SQLite, or a purpose-built format?

SQLite, range-read. The measurement in §4 settles it: the bundle is ~10 MB, and a purpose-built page format only earns its keep when the file is far larger than that. What you get for free is worth more than the bytes a bespoke format would save — one file for both platforms, a real query planner, indexes, and a format the mobile side needs anyway. A hand-rolled format means writing and testing two readers, and doc 13 §0.5 is a page of evidence about what happens when there are two of something.

The thing to get right is not the format, it is the page layout. Range-reading is fast when the rows a query wants sit in adjacent pages. So the web packager writes tables in the order the queries read them: rule and rule_section clustered by item_id, so one reach's rules land in one or two 64 KB reads. If regsForItem needs more than about four range requests, the layout is wrong — not the format.

One data type or two?

Two, and that is fine. They optimise for opposite things and they are both cheap to produce because they are pure functions of the same content store:

Charting

No charting library. react-native-svg and draw it ourselves.

  • It must render under react-native, react-native-web and desktop DOM. That rules out anything canvas-based, which is most of them — the archived hydrograph needed chart.js plus a date adapter plus a zoom plugin, three dependencies for one chart.
  • react-native-svg is in the Expo SDK 57 bundle, so expo install manages its version — the thing deps.md says to care about.
  • The hydrograph is the only chart in the app, and it is an unusual one: a percentile envelope, a log axis, two year traces and forecast ribbons. Generic chart APIs fight all four.
  • Rule 25 keeps it honest — the scales, ticks and paths are computed in a hook in @app/ui; the component only draws the shapes it is handed. So the desktop version is a different renderer over the same arithmetic, not a second chart.

The one measurement still outstanding

How big is the trimmed tile set? v1's numbers give the ceiling — 807 MB of streams, 4.36 GB of basemap — but neither was built with the magnitude ladder, the pruned reaches, or a trimmed basemap layer list. Everything in §8 assumes the province fits comfortably on a phone. Build the two tile sets for Region 2 at the §3 ladder and extrapolate; it is a day's work and it is the only number left that could change the shape of the answer.

What this document deliberately does not decide

Shard boundaries, page size, bitmap encoding, exact tile zoom cut-points and search ranking are packager decisions (doc 13 §3). Putting them here would leak packing into the content model — the mistake that made v1's dense ids renumber the whole file on every build. They belong in the two packagers, behind the interface in §7, tested by the one conformance suite.
